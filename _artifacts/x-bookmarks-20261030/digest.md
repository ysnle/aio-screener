I'm dumping the majority of my Intel shares. This was genuinely one of the saddest earnings calls.

But after listening to the entire earnings call, I honestly think Intel messed up BAD. And what makes this even more frustrating is that Intel has one of the biggest opportunities in the semiconductor industry sitting right in front of it, yet it cannot fully take advantage of it.

First, the guidance was absolutely terrible. Intel reported $16.5 billion in Q3 revenue and adjusted EPS of $0.52, which were actually pretty solid results. But then management guided for just $15 billion to $16 billion in Q4 revenue, compared to Wall Street's expectations of around $17.1 billion. They also expect adjusted EPS to fall to $0.25, compared to expectations of roughly $0.43. Even worse, gross margins are expected to fall from 44.1% to just 38% in a single quarter. That's a massive decline, especially for a company that's supposed to be improving its profitability.

But the biggest problem is what management said about 2027. Intel's PC business is getting destroyed by the ongoing memory shortage. Companies like Samsung, SK Hynix, and Micron are benefiting from enormous AI demand, but that same demand is making memory more expensive and harder to find for traditional computers.

And when memory becomes more expensive, the entire computer becomes more expensive. That means fewer people and businesses are willing to upgrade their PCs, which directly hurts Intel. Management now expects the overall PC market to shrink by around 12% in 2026 and another 10% in 2027. Intel also expects its own client revenue to fall more than 20% in Q4 alone. What really caught my attention was when management basically admitted that they don't expect the memory situation to improve in 2027. So this isn't just a temporary problem that disappears next quarter. It could continue hurting Intel for another year or longer.

Now, here's what frustrates me the MOST. Intel's data center business is absolutely booming, but they cannot manufacture enough chips to meet demand. Data Center and AI revenue grew nearly 80% year over year to $7.3 billion. Management expects industry server CPU shipments to grow more than 30% in both 2026 and 2027, and Lip-Bu Tan even believes the server CPU market could grow from around $40 billion today to more than $200 billion over the next five years because of AI agents. That's an unbelievable opportunity. But Intel is dealing with manufacturing capacity limitations, production bottlenecks, and shortages of the materials needed to package its chips. In other words, customers WANT to buy more Intel server CPUs, but Intel cannot produce enough of them.

And management made it pretty clear that these supply problems will continue into the first half of 2027, with meaningful improvements not expected until the second half. This is what bothers me more than anything. We're experiencing one of the strongest periods of CPU demand in years, and Intel is leaving billions of dollars in potential sales on the table while competitors fight for market share. Then there's the tragic accident at Intel's factory in Israel, which forced production to shut down for approximately two weeks. Management expects this to reduce Q4 revenue by around $300 million, with a similar impact potentially extending into Q1. It also accounts for about two percentage points of the expected Q4 gross margin decline. Obviously, this was an unfortunate situation, but it creates even more problems for a company that was already struggling to produce enough chips.

And don't even get me started on the foundry business. This was supposed to be one of the biggest reasons to invest in Intel. The idea was that Intel could become a serious competitor to TSMC by manufacturing chips for other companies. And yes, Intel is making progress. Its 18A manufacturing process is improving, production costs are coming down, and management sounds more confident about 14A but here's the reality. Intel's foundry business still lost $1.9 BILLION in Q3. External foundry revenue was only $411 million, and part of that came from a one time customer program. Management keeps talking about potential customers, improving yields, and future opportunities. But where are the major external customer commitments that will actually make this business profitable? I'm not saying they won't come. I'm saying investors have already been waiting years for this turnaround, and we're still talking about what MIGHT happen in 2027 and 2028.

And then came another comment that really disappointed me. Intel doesn't expect to generate positive free cash flow in 2027. The company already expects to spend around $20 billion on capital expenditures in 2026, and management said spending will increase significantly next year. So we're looking at another year of massive investments, continued foundry losses, a declining PC market, and potentially negative free cash flow. And remember, Intel raised $23 billion by issuing additional shares in August, meaning existing shareholders also have to deal with dilution. At some point, investors need to ask when all this spending is actually going to translate into sustainable profits.

Granite Rapids is becoming more profitable, its custom silicon business is growing rapidly, and its upcoming products like Diamond Rapids and Nova Lake could help it regain market share. I also think the long term opportunity in AI infrastructure, advanced packaging, and foundry services is enormous. But here's the issue. Almost every major positive Intel talks about is something that could happen in the FUTURE. More manufacturing capacity in the second half of 2027. Potential foundry customers. A stronger product roadmap. Better profitability. Eventually positive free cash flow.

Meanwhile, all the problems are happening RIGHT NOW. The PC business is declining right now. Manufacturing constraints are hurting sales right now. Foundry is losing billions right now. And margins are expected to fall significantly next quarter. The worst part is that Intel doesn't even have a demand problem in its server business. It has an execution problem. And that's arguably even more frustrating because the opportunity is already there.

I understand that rebuilding a company like Intel takes years, and I still respect what Lip-Bu Tan is trying to accomplish. But after the massive rally in this stock, investors were expecting Intel to finally start delivering meaningful results, not another year of excuses and promises. I was willing to be patient with Intel because I believed the turnaround was gaining momentum. But this earnings call genuinely changed my perspective.

I'm not selling because I suddenly believe Intel is a terrible company or that its technology has no future. I'm selling because I no longer think the risk and reward are nearly as attractive as they were before. I'm dumping the majority of my position, but I'll likely keep a smaller stake because I still believe there's long-term potential here. I really wanted Intel to succeed. I still do. But wanting a company to succeed and believing its stock is a good investment at the current valuation are two completely different things.

And after listening to management today, I just cannot justify maintaining the same level of exposure. Intel had the opportunity to prove that its turnaround was finally working. Instead, management gave investors even more reasons to question it. This was genuinely disappointing.

What is the 3-Day Rule, and why does it matter? Let me explain.

When a stock gaps down sharply after earnings or bad news, wait at least 3 trading days before considering buying.

Days 2 and 3: Selling pressure may continue, and the stock could make new lows as investors reassess the news.

After Day 3: Look for signs that selling pressure is fading and price is beginning to stabilize.

Before buying, check:
- Selling volume is shrinking
- Price is holding above the recent low
- You understand why the stock dropped
- The fundamental thesis remains intact

If the business is fundamentally broken, the stock isn't necessarily on sale. It may simply be getting repriced.

My key takeaways from the $GOOGL (Alphabet) Q3 2026 earnings call.

1. Search slowed while query growth hit a five-year high. Search and other ads grew 12% to $63.3B, down from Q2. The deceleration has four parts. FX was the biggest drag. The EU ended its de minimis exemption (duty-free treatment for cheap parcels), which hit APAC retail advertisers. World Cup media budgets landed in June instead of July. And AI Mode queries grew fast on a surface with "lower monetization" than classic Search. The first three wash out over time. The fourth is structural: more usage on a surface that earns less per query.

2. The EPS miss is mostly a SpaceX mark. Revenue grew 25% to $127.6B. Operating income grew 30% to $40.6B, a 31.8% margin. EPS came in at $2.12 against $3.02 expected because OI&E (other income and expense, the non-operating line where investment gains and losses land) swung to negative $8.2B. Most of that was an unrealized loss on the SpaceX stake after its share price fell. My rough math: after tax, that mark costs around $0.55 a share, so it explains more than half the $0.90 gap but not all of it.

3. Q4 Search is already guided softer. Lapping last year's Search acceleration cost about 3 points of growth in Q3 and will cost a couple more in Q4. FX gets slightly worse at current spot rates, mostly in Search and YouTube. The rest of Services won't make up the difference: YouTube ads grew only 9% to $11.2B, and Network grew 1% to $7.5B.

4. The AI Mode ad fix is rolling out but not proven. Ads in AI Mode go international for the first time, and multi-turn ads (ads that stay in a back-and-forth conversation) launch in the U.S. The evidence offered: inside AI Mode, sponsored and organic product listings get similar click-through rates. AI Mode queries run about 3x longer than classic searches, and most are incremental, meaning they would not have happened otherwise. What's missing is any number on how far AI Mode revenue per query trails classic Search. Agentic commerce (AI agents that shop and buy for users) is called additive, and consumer agents arrive in the Gemini app later this year. No revenue is attached to it yet.

5. Cloud doubled. Revenue grew 103% to $30.7B. Operating income was $11.1B, more than triple last year. Margin was 36.2% versus 23.7% a year ago. Backlog (signed contracts not yet booked as revenue) reached $561B, and just over 55% of it, about $310B, converts within 24 months. Two caveats. TPU system sales contributed to the growth but were not sized, and Cloud growth excluding TPUs was described as very strong with no number attached. Also, the rented third-party compute capacity came online only at the very end of Q3, so its margin drag lands in Q4.

6. TPU economics stay private. A TPU is Google's in-house AI chip, sold here as full racks with networking, storage and software. One analyst framed a TPU system at roughly $35B per gigawatt and asked how much of that becomes revenue and at what margin. Neither got an answer, because product-level financials are not disclosed. The cash cycle is clear. A signed contract shows up in backlog, Google builds inventory, and cash arrives on delivery. Inventory is only built against committed contracts. There are nearly 100 TPU customers, including Anthropic (directly and through Broadcom) and Apple, which is scaling pretraining on TPUs. New distribution comes through Oracle and Crux AI, a joint venture with Blackstone. The meaningful revenue ramp is 2027.

7. Free cash flow went negative. Q3 FCF was negative $9.1B on $51.4B of capex. Operating cash flow of $42.3B also absorbed the TPU inventory build and a $5.2B payment on a 2018 EC fine. Trailing twelve-month FCF is $19.7B. The 2026 capex guide is unchanged at $195B to $205B, and 2027 rises significantly, with numbers on the Q4 call. On top of that sit $924B in future purchase commitments and $152B in leases that haven't started and other obligations. Those are contractual maximums, and some have volume flexibility.

8. The return math is stated, not shown. Return on invested capital is said to stay meaningfully above the cost of capital, with no figure. Compute allocation is described as a long-range plan run on ROIC discipline, with no ranking of who gets capacity first. Cloud says it could serve a lot more demand with more capacity. About 65% of Q3 infrastructure spend went to servers and networking depreciated over six years, while TPU 8t claims up to 2.7x the training performance of the prior generation. My view: six years is a long life for hardware that loses that much ground each generation. Shorter lives would mean higher depreciation on top of the capex.

While Claude Code already lets you customize a lot of its behavior, such as configuration settings, permission policies, slash commands, skills and status lines, mods allow you to do so much more. With mods you can alter or override the behavior of Claude Code or render your own custom interfaces. Folks have been having fun building mods, including multiplayer Doom!

How mods work
At a technical level, mods are hooks bundled with plugins. Each hook can see all events occurring within the session, such as when tool calls are made, prompts are submitted, turns start and end, sessions start and end, slash commands are executed, and every rendering of any part of the UI.
Hooks are chained together like middleware. When an event occurs, it is passed through your hook, then through other plugins, before finally hitting Claude Code and performing its default action.
 
A hook can do one of three things when an event occurs:
Observe: Allow the event to continue and observe the outcome. For instance, a hook could log every time a file is modified or take a snapshot after every turn.
Rewrite: Change the event for the remaining hooks. For instance, a hook could add text to a prompt or change a tool call's input.
Answer: Handle the event itself, so the default behavior doesn't run. For instance, a hook could deny a tool call or answer a command.
These terms are helpful when thinking about what a mod does and how to ask Claude for one: "Refuse this kind of command" is an answer, whereas "Take a snapshot after every turn" is an observation.
What makes mods different from settings hooks
A settings hook is a shell command that runs each time an event occurs. A mod is loaded once into a session and maintains its own state and UI which it can update in response to events. A mod can also call back into Claude Code. For example, it can open a pane, spawn a process, or register a slash command or tool that's callable by the model.
We use mods to implement some of Claude Code's own features, such as AGENTS.md support and the /diff pane. The code and tests are available in the public anthropics/claude-code repo in the mods/ directory.
